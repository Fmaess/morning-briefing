Morning, big dog. Coffee in hand? Good. Let's do this.

It's Saturday, the thirty-first of October, and the theme is simple: a tech-led selloff in the US collides with oil at six-month highs, so you walk into Monday with Wall Street wobbling and energy bid.

Markets are shut for the weekend, so everything I give you is Friday's close. Let's start with Asia.

Japan took the hit. The Nikkei fell two point one percent to sixty-three thousand, two hundred ninety, though it's still up almost one percent on the week. The Bank of Japan added to the mood. It will speed up its ETF sales, more than doubling the pace from January. Governor Ueda also said the two percent inflation goal has not yet been sustainably achieved. The yen sat near one hundred fifty-eight to the dollar, basically flat on the day.

Hong Kong went the other way. The Hang Seng rose one point five percent to just over twenty-three thousand. Australia's ASX 200 added a modest point two percent.

Then mainland China, where the signals were soft. The Shanghai Composite dropped one point four percent and is down two and a half percent on the week. Factory activity contracted in October, the third contraction in four months. One headline called it a faltering recovery. The yuan is steady at about six point six five per dollar. Xinhua did report a sharp increase in gold, nickel and strategic minerals, so Beijing is stocking up on hard assets.

Now the US, and here's the thing: it was ugly. The S&P 500 fell one point eight percent to seventy-five hundred eighty. The Nasdaq 100 got hit much harder, down three and a half percent to twenty-eight thousand, three hundred eighty-five. That's the Nasdaq's first three-week losing streak since March. The VIX jumped nearly thirteen percent to nineteen point four.

What's behind it? Concerning projections from the hyperscalers this week. The worry is free cash flow and rising borrowing costs hitting the AI trade. S&P Global Ratings gave Meta a negative outlook on capital spending and cash flow concerns. Futures were mirroring the cash market on Friday: S&P futures at seventy-six hundred fourteen, down one point eight percent, and Nasdaq futures down three point five.

On to rates. Treasuries rallied. The ten-year yield fell six basis points to four point nine three percent, and the five-year dropped eight basis points to four point seven two. So money ran to bonds, but yields are still high, and that's the squeeze on long-duration tech. In Europe, the Bund move was small, using the Bundesbank's prior-day figures. The two-year sits at three point oh six percent and the ten-year at three point three eight, each down one basis point.

Commodities. Brent rose two point four percent to one hundred seventeen dollars and sixty-four cents a barrel, a six-month high. The driver is Hormuz. Attacks there threaten the Iran peace efforts. Axios reports Qatari mediators held more than twelve hours of talks in Tehran on Friday to avoid a resumption of American-led attacks on Iran. So the headline risk cuts both ways: a deal could drop oil fast, and a breakdown could push it higher.

European gas jumped five point four percent, up nearly eleven percent on the week, to about one hundred four. Gold fell three point three percent to thirty-eight ninety-nine an ounce, a surprise in a risk-off tape. One datapoint to note is that India's gold imports are reported down eighty-one percent. Copper slipped one point nine percent. The euro edged up to one point one one eight three against the dollar.

Earnings. A thin slate since yesterday, but an important one. Exxon Mobil reported earnings of four dollars twenty-three a share against an estimate of four oh four, a beat of nearly five percent. The shares rose about two percent. Alongside that, CNBC points out that oil majors have posted surging refining earnings as fuel supplies tightened, even after Trump said Big Oil was making too much money. That's one report, so I won't over-read it, but it fits the tape: tight fuel markets, strong energy cash flows.

Now the cross-currents. Two stories sit side by side. In the US, the market is punishing AI spending where the cash flow is stretched. Meanwhile in Asia, the hardware suppliers are printing money. Kioxia expects net profit to surge fifteen-fold to eight point seven billion dollars this quarter. Memory chip makers are out-earning Alphabet and other tech giants. Murata plans to invest six point three billion dollars in capacitors for AI over five years. And Foxconn's chief downplays bubble concerns and sees strong growth in 2027. So demand for AI hardware looks real. The debate is who pays for it, and at what cost of capital. In other words, it's about funding and returns, not about demand.

The second theme is energy. Higher fuel prices are feeding refiners' profits, and at the same time they're squeezing consumers and politicians. A headline today notes that gasoline, diesel and jet fuel soared despite promises to cut them. With the midterms near, that matters. The third is tariffs: Trump is reported to be holding off overcapacity tariffs as the midterms approach. That's a small relief for exporters, but only a delay.

So what does it mean for Europe? Friday's close was already soft. The Stoxx 600 fell point three six percent to six forty-six point seven five, but it's still up one point seven percent on the week. The DAX lost point eight five percent to twenty-five thousand, eight hundred eighteen, and the Euro Stoxx 50 fell one point two percent to sixty-one thirty-one.

Now, the rule of thumb. Historically, the Stoxx 600 has tended to follow Wall Street with a lag. After an S&P drop like this one, the model points to roughly twenty-one basis points of weakness for the Stoxx 600. That's a historical pattern, not a forecast, and it explains about a fifth of the variation. Treat it as a tilt, not a call.

Sector by sector, the same model says oil and gas has historically outperformed the market by about twenty-five basis points on days like this, and basic resources by about twenty-six. Both fits are weak, so lean on the logic more than the numbers. The logic for energy is strong: Brent at one hundred seventeen, gas climbing, and Exxon's beat. That's the clearest tailwind. For miners, I'd be more careful. China's factory data is weak and copper fell, even if Beijing is stocking up on strategic minerals.

The headwinds, in my view, are European names tied to the AI capex chain, if Nasdaq weakness carries over on Monday. Energy-intensive industry also faces the gas spike, and this could weigh on margins. China-exposed exporters have another soft PMI to digest, though Swiss firms are reportedly planning to expand investments there as a trade deal deepens ties. One local risk is France, which is bracing for fresh unrest while the government vows a tough response. I'd watch French assets for headline noise.

Watch the Iran talks and Brent first. The jobs report, Apple and AMD earnings, and the US midterms follow next week.

One line to take away: in a market worried about who pays for AI, the cleanest cash flows are in energy.

That's your edge for today. Use it wisely, champ.
