Morning, champ! It's Thursday, the fifth of November, and the thesis is simple: the midterm blue wave removed political uncertainty and crushed the oil price, but it also put a question mark over the AI buildout, and that tension is what splits the tape this morning.

Start with the US. Democrats took Congress, flipping seven Senate seats and nearly thirty House districts. Markets read that as a divided Washington, so fewer policy shocks. The S&P 500 jumped one point six percent, the Nasdaq 100 rose nearly one percent, and the VIX collapsed by twenty percent to sixteen point six. Gold gained as the dollar dipped, and the euro is up half a percent at one point one three.

But here is the complication. Oil fell hard. Vice President Vance blamed the Iran war for the losses, and Brent dropped below ninety dollars on the day. This morning it sits at ninety-four, still down eighteen percent over five days. Lower oil eases margins and inflation, so the US ten-year yield fell almost six basis points to four point nine percent. Fine. But the same election hit the data-center story. Texas results raised long-term questions about the AI buildout, and chip names like Micron slipped, along with power providers like Vistra. Analysts warn that's an excessive knee-jerk, since the vote doesn't unwind projects already funded. Meanwhile the hyperscalers and Apple rallied on lower uncertainty.

So the market is rotating inside tech, not just in or out. Nasdaq futures are down one point three percent this morning, and that's the signal.

Asia shows it. The Kospi collapsed five point two percent, and it's down more than nine percent over five days. Korea is the chip and memory proxy, so it is where AI doubt lands hardest. The Nikkei fell one point eight percent. Qualcomm's seven percent drop adds to it: decent results, weak guidance, as a memory shortage and high memory costs squeeze smartphone demand. Figma slid too, because AI features cost real money to deliver. AMD, though, nearly doubled data center revenue. So demand is real, but the market now asks who pays for it.

[pause]

And Hong Kong ripped. The Hang Seng rose three point three percent, up more than seven and a half percent on the week, while Shanghai slipped slightly. Australia gained two point six percent, helped by NextDC surging forty-three percent after Goodman and Digital Realty bought stakes. Note the irony: data-center assets are being bought even as data-center stocks sell off.

Now Europe, which closed up almost two percent yesterday, the DAX up two point two. Futures point softer today, but the historical rule of thumb is mildly positive for the Stoxx 600, around plus nineteen basis points. It is a rule of thumb, not a forecast. The main driver is the Hang Seng's strength, partly offset by the Nikkei's weakness and yesterday's oil drop.

On sectors, history suggests luxury could outperform by around ninety basis points, as Hong Kong strength tends to lift it. Autos tend to follow, plus forty-one. BMW reported earnings per share of two point four six against one point seven one expected, a forty-four percent beat, yet the stock is down one point eight percent, with headlines pointing to a margin cut. So the beat is in the number, but the market is looking at quality. Toyota raised its profit forecast on hybrids despite the China slump, a useful read-across.

Healthcare tends to outperform too, about fifty-one basis points, as Korea weakness drives defensive rotation. Novo Nordisk beat by almost eighteen percent and improved its outlook, yet the stock is flat. Good news was priced in. Utilities and technology tend to lag, minus forty and minus thirty-nine basis points. Energy is slightly negative, which fits: oil is lower, though BP and Baker Hughes executives talk about a demand decade.

Rates matter too. Bund yields barely moved, with the ten-year at three point three five percent. The Bank of England is poised to hike for the first time in three years, and the Bank of Japan minutes show a debate about faster moves. So global policy is tightening at the edges even as oil relief arrives.

What would change the picture? A Korea stabilization would calm the tech rotation. A rebound in oil would hit the margin story. And the debt-ceiling fight with a Democratic Congress could bring volatility back.

[pause]

The take-away: the election removed the noise, but it exposed the fault line in AI, so stay selective in Europe.

Briefing over. Go show the market who's boss.
