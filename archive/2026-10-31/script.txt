Morning, big dog. Coffee in hand? Good. Let's do this.

It's Saturday, October thirty-first, and the dominant theme is simple: AI-capex fatigue meets expensive oil, and tech took the hit.

Let's start in the US, where Friday was ugly. The Nasdaq 100 fell three and a half percent, the S&P 500 dropped one point eight. That leaves the Nasdaq with its first three-week losing streak since March. The trigger, according to the headlines, was worrying projections from the hyperscalers this week, plus S&P Global putting Meta on negative outlook over heavy capex and free cash flow. That's the chain: the AI trade has been funded by cash flow, cash flow is shrinking as spending climbs, and borrowing costs are rising. So the market is asking who pays for all this, and what the return is.

Asia felt it too. The Kospi fell three and a half percent, even after a strong week. That looks like profit-taking in a crowded, memory-heavy market. The Nikkei lost two point one percent. Shanghai slipped one point four, and the soft spot is real: China's factory activity contracted in October, the third contraction in four months. Hong Kong was the exception, up one point five percent.

Now the second leg: energy. Brent rose two point four percent to one hundred seventeen dollars sixty a barrel, a six-month high, as attacks around Hormuz threaten the Iran peace effort. European gas is the sharper signal. TTF rose five point four percent on the day and almost eleven percent over the week, to about one hundred four. For Europe, that matters more than the crude price. It means higher input costs for industry, margin pressure, and stickier inflation.

Here's the interesting part. Normally, an equity sell-off like this pulls yields down, and it did: the US ten-year fell six basis points to four point nine percent, and the five-year fell eight. But a yield near five percent is still a heavy valuation weight on long-duration tech, and with oil this high, the market can't count on easy relief. Bunds barely moved, down one basis point at the ten-year to three point four percent. So Europe is not getting a big rates cushion.

Gold is the odd one out. It fell three point three percent, to about thirty-nine hundred dollars, which is not what you'd expect in a risk-off day. That suggests some forced selling, people raising cash from winners. The VIX jumped almost thirteen percent to nineteen point four. That's elevated, not panic. Copper dipped one point nine percent, consistent with the soft China data. The euro edged up to one point one two against the dollar.

Earnings were thin. ExxonMobil reported earnings of four dollars twenty-three a share against four oh four expected, a beat of almost five percent, and the stock rose about two percent. Beyond the beat, the headlines point to surging refining earnings across the majors as fuel supplies tighten. That's a pricing-power story, and it's politically loaded, given the criticism of Big Oil profits ahead of the midterms.

So, Europe on Monday. Our historical rule of thumb, and it's only that, points to a softer start for the Stoxx 600 after a weak US close. The model shows an expected drag of about twenty-one basis points, driven by the S&P move, though the explanatory power is modest. Historically, energy has tended to outperform the market on days like this, and so have commodity-linked names. So the energy complex looks like a relative tailwind, with gas and oil both climbing.

Headwinds? Tech and semiconductor-capex names, especially anything tied to the hyperscaler spending story. Note that Europe held up better on Friday, with the Stoxx 600 down only point four percent and a solid week behind it. That means less de-risking has happened here, and some positioning may still be exposed to catch-down. Energy-intensive industrials face the gas squeeze. Exporters with China exposure face weak demand data, though Swiss firms reportedly plan to expand there, so it isn't uniform.

Defensives are the likely destination for rotation. The open question is whether that's tactical positioning ahead of the US midterms and the jobs report, or a more structural shift away from capex-heavy growth. One data point on the structural side: memory chip makers reportedly outearn some tech giants, so AI demand isn't collapsing. The debate is about who captures the profits and who funds the spending.

Other risks to watch: France bracing for fresh unrest, and Qatari mediators pushing to restart US-Iran talks. A deal would pull oil lower and flip the energy trade fast. Failure does the opposite.

The takeaway: this is a funding story, not a demand story. Expensive oil and gas plus expensive money are testing the AI trade.

That's your edge for today. Use it wisely, champ.
