Morning, big dog. Coffee in hand? Good. Let's do this.

Today's thesis: the AI spending scare is colliding with a six-month high in oil, and that combination is what turned a promising week into a rough Friday.

It's Saturday, October thirty-first, so this is your look back at Friday's close and what it means for Monday's setup in Europe.

Start with the tech selloff. Big Tech earnings this week reignited fears about AI spending, and the Nasdaq 100 fell three and a half percent on Friday. That's the Nasdaq's first three-week losing streak since March. The S&P 500 lost one point eight percent. Nasdaq futures are down about the same, so there was no bounce into the weekend.

But here's the twist. The same Asian tape that was selling tech also carried the counterargument. Memory chip makers are out-earning Alphabet and other tech giants on AI demand. Kioxia is guiding to a fifteen-fold jump in quarterly net profit, to eight point seven billion dollars. Murata is putting six point three billion into capacitor capacity for AI. Foxconn's chief is downplaying bubble talk and sees strong growth in 2027. So the suppliers are printing money, but the market is asking whether the customers can keep paying for it. The Kospi, with its memory exposure, fell three and a half percent on the day, even though it's still up three point two percent over five days. That looks like a crowded winner being sold, not a broken story.

[pause]

Now the complication. Normally a fall like this would bring relief from oil and rates. Rates did help: the US ten-year yield fell six basis points to four point nine percent, and the five-year dropped eight. But oil went the other way. Brent rose two point four percent to one hundred seventeen dollars sixty-four, a six-month high, as attacks around Hormuz threaten the Iran peace effort. Qatari mediators spent more than twelve hours in Tehran on Friday trying to restart direct US-Iran talks. That's the swing factor.

So oil near one hundred eighteen dollars eats into margins and keeps inflation sticky. That limits how far central banks can ease, even when growth fears pull yields lower. And Europe is more exposed than the US. TTF gas jumped five point four percent to just over one hundred four euros, and it's up almost eleven percent in a week. That's a direct hit to industrial input costs.

Then there's China, which doesn't help. Factory activity shrank in October, the third contraction in four months. Shanghai fell one point four percent, and copper slipped one point nine percent. Interesting detail: China reported a sharp rise in gold, nickel and strategic mineral figures, and Chinese shipping and coal stocks are outperforming AI names as earnings outlooks diverge. Money is moving from the growth story to the hard-asset and cash-flow story.

Even gold didn't hold up as a haven. It fell three point three percent to just under three thousand nine hundred dollars. India's gold imports are reportedly down eighty-one percent, a demand hole. Add the VIX jumping nearly thirteen percent to nineteen point four, and you get a market that is nervous but not panicking.

Now, Europe. Friday's close was already soft. The Euro Stoxx 50 lost one point two percent, the DAX eight-tenths of a percent, and the Stoxx 600 just over a third of a percent. But look at the five-day numbers: the DAX is up two and a half percent. Europe went into this selloff with gains to protect.

The historical rule of thumb, which is not a forecast, points to a Stoxx 600 about twenty basis points lower, driven by the S&P's drop. The same model says energy and commodity names have tended to outperform on days like this, by roughly twenty-five basis points versus the market. The fit is weak, so treat it as a lean, not a call.

So what's the setup? Tailwinds: energy, where oil above one hundred fifteen dollars is lifting refining earnings, as the majors have already shown. Headwinds: European tech and semicap names tied to AI orders, and energy-intensive industrials facing gas near one hundred four euros. Defensives like healthcare are the natural parking spot. Whether that's temporary positioning or a lasting rotation depends on the next round of AI capex commentary.

What changes the picture? A breakthrough in the Qatari-mediated talks would pull oil lower and ease the squeeze on margins. A fresh Hormuz escalation does the opposite. And in the background, the Bank of Japan is doubling its ETF selling pace from January, a slow supply overhang for Japanese equities.

[pause]

The takeaway: AI doubts hit the growth side, oil hits the cost side, and Europe is stuck in the middle.

That's your edge for today. Use it wisely, champ.
