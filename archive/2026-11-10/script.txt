Wakey wakey, champ. Futures are moving.

It's Tuesday, the tenth of November, and the thesis is simple: a falling oil price just handed risk assets a relief rally, but the bounce is only as good as today's inflation print.

Let's build it. Oil is the first domino. Brent is down about one percent this morning at ninety-one dollars and change, and it is down nearly twelve percent over five days. Cheaper crude eases the margin squeeze and takes pressure off inflation. Asia took that and ran. The Kospi jumped four percent, the Nikkei rose two point seven percent, and SoftBank surged thirteen percent as semiconductor buying spilled over from Wall Street.

But look at the context. The Kospi is still down seven and a half percent over five days, and the Nikkei is down four percent. So this is a bounce inside a bruising week, not a clean new trend. The Bank of Japan is part of that tension. Its October summary shows policymakers debated faster rate hikes, while the dollar-yen rate sits near one fifty-eight. A central bank leaning hawkish into a market rout is not a comfortable mix.

On Wall Street, the S&P 500 rose two point four percent yesterday and the Nasdaq 100 gained three point four. The VIX collapsed twenty-two percent to just under twenty. Futures are flat this morning, so the bounce is being digested, not extended.

[pause]

Here is the complication. Yields did not follow oil down. The US ten-year is at four point nine percent, and the Bund ten-year is at three point three percent, up three basis points. The German two-year rose four basis points to three percent. So equities are celebrating cheaper oil, but bonds are still pricing sticky inflation and tighter funding. That is why today's US consumer price report matters. Expectations are for a slower pace of price increases in October. If it confirms that, the rally has a floor. If it surprises higher, then oil's relief gets overwhelmed by rates, and the rotation trade into cyclicals and small caps gets tested first.

And there is a second wobble underneath. Nvidia is in the headlines over the allegations around Jensen Huang and a Chinese spy, plus scrutiny of its chip buyer Megaspeed. Analysts warn this could be a longer-lasting problem. But money is moving sideways rather than out: AMD and Broadcom gained, while strategists flag that Washington has gone from tailwind to potential headwind after the midterms. So the AI trade is not dead. It is being repriced for governance and political risk.

China adds another layer. October exports jumped thirty-five percent, and the trade surplus has crossed one trillion dollars this year. That says global demand for Chinese goods is strong, but it also says pricing pressure and trade friction are not going away. For Europe, that means competition for industrials and autos, while strong Chinese trade supports volumes. The Hang Seng is up modestly, Shanghai is flat, and gold is up about four point eight percent on the week at over four thousand one hundred dollars. Some investors are still buying insurance.

Now Europe. The Stoxx 600 rose one point three percent yesterday, with banks and defence leading a broad rally. The historical rule of thumb says the Stoxx 600 could open about half a percent higher, helped by Wall Street and Japan, and trimmed slightly by oil. That is a tendency, not a forecast.

Earnings fit the banks story. UniCredit reported third-quarter earnings per share of two point one five euros against an estimate of one eighty-eight, a beat of fourteen percent, and highlighted record profit. And yet the stock is down about half a percent. So the good news was priced in. With banks already leading, the market is paying for perfection. With higher yields helping margins, that is the crowded trade to watch if inflation comes in soft and yields drop.

Sector by sector, history suggests industrials tend to outperform when Japan rallies, which is a plus for the cyclical story. Telecom, utilities and real estate have tended to lag on days like this, because defensives get sold when risk appetite returns. Oil and gas has tended to underperform with a strong S&P and falling crude, so energy is the clear headwind. Technology looks roughly neutral, with Japan's rally offset by the oil effect. Healthcare is also flat, though a failed heart drug trial casts a shadow over peers developing similar therapies.

One more swing factor: peace talks on Ukraine begin today in Abu Dhabi, with President Trump signaling a strong inclination to end the war. Progress could weigh on defence names that have led, while helping energy-sensitive and consumer sectors. A failure does the reverse.

What would change the picture? A hot inflation number, a renewed oil spike, or more Nvidia fallout.

[pause]

Cheaper oil lit the fuse, but inflation decides whether it burns.

Briefing over. Go show the market who's boss.
