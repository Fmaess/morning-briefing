Morning, champ! It's Saturday, the thirty-first of October, and the theme heading into Monday is a tech sell-off driven by AI spending worries, colliding with oil at six-month highs.

Markets are shut today, so everything here is as of Friday's close. Monday's open is the next real test.

Let's start in Asia, where Friday was a mixed and fairly nervous session. The Nikkei fell two point one percent to sixty-three thousand, two hundred and ninety. Even so, it is still up almost one percent on the week. The Kospi was the weak spot, down three point five percent. That's a sharp drop, but the index is still up three point two percent over five days. The Hang Seng went the other way, up one point five percent to twenty-three thousand, two hundred and ninety-four. Shanghai fell one point four percent and is down two and a half percent on the week. Australia was quiet, up zero point two percent.

China's data explains some of the Shanghai weakness. Factory activity shrank in October, the third contraction in four months, and the reports describe a recovery that is faltering. A separate report from Xinhua points to a sharp increase in gold, nickel and strategic minerals. I'd read that as a sign Beijing is stocking up on strategic materials, though that's my interpretation, not something the report says. On the currency side, the yen is around one hundred fifty-seven point eight against the dollar, and the yuan is at six point six five.

One more item from Japan. The Bank of Japan is speeding up its sales of exchange-traded funds, more than doubling the pace from January. Governor Ueda also said the two percent inflation goal has not yet been sustainably achieved. That's a cautious message, and the yen barely reacted.

Now the US, which is the main story. Friday was ugly. The S&P 500 fell one point eight percent to seventy-five hundred and eighty. The Nasdaq 100 dropped three and a half percent, and it is down two percent on the week. The Nasdaq has now had its first three-week losing streak since March. The VIX jumped almost thirteen percent to nineteen point four.

Futures are in the same place, with the S&P future down one point eight percent and the Nasdaq future down three and a half percent. They haven't traded since the cash close, so there's no fresh weekend signal yet.

The reasons are all in the headlines. Hyperscaler projections this week raised concerns about free cash flow and capital spending. S&P Global gave Meta a negative outlook on exactly those points, heavy capex and cash flow. Commentators are already comparing the Nasdaq's rough ride to the dot-com top in 2000. That's an opinion piece, not a fact, but it shows the mood. Borrowing costs are part of the story too.

On rates, though, Friday helped a little. The US ten-year yield fell six basis points to four point nine three percent. The five-year dropped eight basis points to four point seven two. So this was a flight to bonds, not a rates shock. German yields barely moved. According to the Bundesbank's figures for the previous day, the two-year Bund yield is three point oh six percent and the ten-year is three point three eight, each down one basis point.

Now commodities, where the energy picture is tense. Brent rose two point four percent to one hundred seventeen dollars and sixty-four cents a barrel, a six-month high. Reports say attacks around Hormuz are threatening the Iran peace effort. There is a diplomatic track, though. Axios reports Qatari mediators held more than twelve hours of talks in Tehran on Friday, aimed at avoiding a resumption of American-led attacks and restarting direct US-Iran negotiations. So the oil price is trading headlines both ways.

European gas is even more eye-catching. The Dutch TTF benchmark jumped five point four percent to just under one hundred and four, and it's up almost eleven percent over five days. That's a real cost problem for European industry.

The metals were weak. Gold fell three point three percent to thirty-eight ninety-nine an ounce. A headline from Nikkei Asia says India's gold imports are down eighty-one percent, and that global prices could be affected. Copper lost one point nine percent. The euro is steady at one point one one eight, up a quarter of a percent.

Now earnings. Since yesterday, there is just one report in the data: Exxon Mobil. Earnings per share came in at four dollars twenty-three against an estimate of four oh four, a beat of nearly five percent. The shares moved up about two percent. A beat like that is no surprise given the oil price. A CNBC piece notes that the oil majors have reported surging refining earnings as fuel supplies tightened, even after Donald Trump said Big Oil was making too much money. That's a political risk worth keeping in the back of your mind for the energy names.

With only one report, I can't build a full cross-section from earnings alone. The wider news flow does give us a few themes, though. First, AI spending is under scrutiny. The market is asking who pays for the capex, and rating agencies are starting to ask the same question. Second, the AI demand story is still very strong in hardware. Nikkei Asia reports that memory chip makers now outearn Alphabet and other tech giants, and Kioxia expects net profit to surge fifteen-fold to eight point seven billion dollars this quarter. Murata plans to invest six point three billion dollars in capacitor production for AI over five years. Foxconn's chief downplays AI bubble concerns and sees strong growth in 2027. So suppliers are earning a lot, while the spenders are being questioned. Third, energy costs are feeding straight into earnings and into politics. Fourth, on tariffs, Nikkei Asia reports Trump is holding off on overcapacity tariffs as the US midterms near. That's a small relief, but it looks like a pause, not a resolution.

What does this mean for Europe on Monday? Europe closed Friday down less than the US. The Stoxx 600 fell zero point four percent to six hundred and forty-seven, the DAX lost zero point eight percent, and the Euro Stoxx 50 fell one point two. The Stoxx 600 is still up one point seven percent on the week. With the Nasdaq down so much, there is a catch-up risk. Historically, on a day after an S&P move like Friday's, the Stoxx 600 has tended to open about twenty-one basis points lower. That's a rule of thumb with modest explanatory power, not a forecast, but the direction fits the picture.

Tailwinds first. Energy is the obvious one. The same rule of thumb says oil and gas has tended to beat the market by about twenty-five basis points after a down day in the S&P. The statistical fit is weak, so don't lean on it too hard. Still, with Brent at a six-month high and refining margins strong, the fundamentals point the same way. The main risk is a sudden Iran deal that knocks oil lower. Resources and miners show a similar historical relative edge of about twenty-six basis points. But gold and copper both fell on Friday and China's factory data was soft, so I'd treat that one with suspicion.

Headwinds. Anything tied to AI spending sentiment looks exposed, such as semiconductors and the equipment makers that supply them. That's my view, not the model's. The question is whether the hyperscaler capex worries hit the suppliers too, or whether the strong memory earnings protect them. Energy-intensive industries also look challenged. Gas near one hundred and four and oil above one hundred seventeen squeeze chemicals, airlines and heavy industry on costs. China-exposed cyclicals, like autos and luxury, face a weak factory picture. On the other side, Swiss firms plan to expand investment in China as their trade deal deepens ties. So there is some supportive news.

One local risk too. Investing.com reports France bracing for fresh unrest, with the government vowing a tough response. We have no detail, so I'd just keep French domestic names on the watch list.

The one-liner. Monday's tension is between falling yields and a shaky AI trade on one side, and expensive energy on the other, so favor energy and be careful with tech.
